# Chapter 6. Why Can Chiral SFG Selectively Probe the First Hydration Shell of DNA?

**The question we want to answer:** There are several layers of water surrounding DNA. Why does the chiral SFG signal come mainly from the first hydration shell, right next to the biomolecule, while water farther away contributes almost nothing?

In the first five chapters, we used symmetry and vibrational coupling to understand the microscopic origin of chiral SFG signals from water. Now we can move on to a more spatial question: **in the complex solution environment surrounding DNA, which water molecules actually contribute to the chiral SFG signal?**

## 6.1 Hydration Water Near the Minor Groove, Major Groove, and Phosphate Backbone

The surface of the B-DNA double helix has three characteristic hydration regions. Water interacts with DNA differently in each of them:

- **Minor groove:** This is a narrow, deep groove. Water molecules fit into the confined space and form highly ordered hydrogen-bond bridges with polar atoms along the edges of the base pairs, such as the nitrogen and oxygen atoms associated with A/T base pairs. Their freedom to move and rearrange is particularly restricted.
- **Major groove:** This groove is more spacious. Water molecules interact with polar sites on the bases and can form multiple layers or extended strands of hydrogen-bond networks. Water in the major groove is generally more mobile than water in the minor groove.
- **Phosphate backbone:** The backbone winds around the outside of the double helix, and each phosphate group carries a negative charge. Water molecules arrange themselves around the backbone by forming hydrogen bonds with the nonbridging phosphate oxygens.

Together, these regions make up the **first hydration shell**: the water molecules that directly interact with DNA through hydrogen bonding.

## 6.2 How Does the DNA Helix Produce Chiral Correlations in These Different Hydration Regions?

Within the first hydration shell, water molecules are not simply attached to DNA at random. Their positions and orientations are directly constrained by the right-handed geometry of the DNA helix and by directional hydrogen bonding.

Experimentally, in 2017, Petersen and colleagues observed a DNA minor-groove-templated **“chiral spine of hydration”** at room temperature using chiral SFG (*ACS Central Science*, 2017). Later, in a series of studies published in 2022 (*Langmuir*) and 2024 (*Journal of Chemical Physics*), Yan and colleagues provided further experimental evidence for a chiral hydration layer at DNA interfaces through isotope substitution, comparisons between mirror-image D-DNA and L-DNA, and phase-resolved measurements.

## 6.3 Long-Range Electrostatic Orientation Is Not the Same as Chiral Correlation

Beyond the first hydration shell—in the second shell and farther out—water molecules may no longer form direct hydrogen bonds with DNA. But they still experience the electrostatic field generated by the negatively charged phosphate backbone.

Here we need to distinguish two different physical effects:

1. **Electrostatic orientation (long-range):** The local electric field generated by DNA's negative charges extends beyond the molecules in direct contact with DNA. It can induce a preferred orientation of the dipoles of more distant water molecules, giving a nonzero average dipole moment, $\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle\neq0$. Such oriented water can contribute significantly to ordinary, achiral SFG signals, such as those measured with ssp polarization.

2. **Transfer of helical chirality (short-range):** As we move away from the DNA surface, thermal motion rapidly weakens the geometric constraints that preserve the helical arrangement of the hydration network. Water molecules farther away may still prefer to point along the electric field, but their spatial distribution becomes statistically mirror-symmetric again. **Being oriented by an electrostatic field does not automatically mean inheriting DNA's helical chirality.** As a result, this oriented water does not produce a net chiral SFG response in psp polarization.

## 6.4 Using MD Simulations to Separate the Contributions of Different Hydration Shells and Regions

An experimental SFG spectrum alone cannot directly tell us which part of the signal comes from water at a particular distance from DNA or in a particular groove. By combining molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with vibrational exciton calculations, however, we can theoretically decompose the total susceptibility of hydration water into contributions from different spatial regions:

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{water}}^{(2)}
=
\chi_{\mathrm{minor}}^{(2)}
+
\chi_{\mathrm{major}}^{(2)}
+
\chi_{\mathrm{backbone}}^{(2)}
+\cdots
$$

Alternatively, we can group the water molecules by their distance from the biomolecular surface and separate the contributions from the first hydration shell, the second hydration shell, and bulk water. This approach appears in two representative studies by the Yan and Hammes-Schiffer groups:

1. **First-hydration-shell selectivity in a protein system (*ACS Central Science*, 2022).** In a system containing an antiparallel $\beta$-sheet peptide ($\mathrm{LK_7\beta}$), the researchers combined chiral SFG experiments with MD simulations and calculated the SFG response from water molecules selected in different spatial regions. They found that the chiral O–H signal came almost entirely from the first hydration shell, where water molecules hydrogen-bond directly to the protein surface. In the second hydration shell and farther away, the chiral correlations in the water network decayed rapidly, and the net contribution to $\chi_{ZYX}^{(2)}$ approached zero.

2. **Confirming first-shell selectivity and separating hydration regions around DNA (*Journal of Chemical Physics*, 2024, **161**, 095104).** For DNA hydration water, the researchers combined phase-resolved chiral SFG experiments with MD simulations. They again found that the chiral water signal was strongly localized to DNA's first hydration shell, while the more distant water oriented by electrostatic fields made almost no net chiral contribution. They then went one step further and separated the first-shell response into contributions from the minor groove, major groove, and phosphate backbone:

   - **Minor-groove water ($\chi_{\mathrm{minor}}^{(2)}$):** Because this region imposes the strongest spatial confinement, water molecules are arranged closely along the helical groove. They produce the strongest chiral SFG response and dominate the sign and intensity of the total spectrum.
   - **Major-groove water ($\chi_{\mathrm{major}}^{(2)}$) and backbone water ($\chi_{\mathrm{backbone}}^{(2)}$):** These regions also contribute nonzero chiral responses. But because their local hydrogen-bond geometries and molecular orientations differ from those in the minor groove, some contributions can have the opposite sign. Positive and negative signals from different regions may therefore partially cancel in the total spectrum.

## 6.5 What We Have Learned, and Where the Explanation Has Its Limits

Putting the experimental and simulation results together, we arrive at the physical reason why chiral SFG can be unusually selective for the first hydration shell of a biomolecule: **chiral geometric correlations depend on short-range directional hydrogen bonding and spatial confinement, while water oriented by a longer-range electrostatic field does not necessarily break mirror symmetry.**

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{First hydration shell (minor groove / major groove / backbone):}\\
\text{short-range hydrogen bonding and spatial confinement}\\
\longrightarrow\text{helical chiral correlations are preserved}\\
\longrightarrow\chi_{ZYX}^{(2)}\ne0\\[4pt]
\text{Second hydration shell and beyond: orientation mainly by longer-range electrostatic fields}\\
\longrightarrow\text{the statistical distribution regains mirror symmetry}\\
\longrightarrow\chi_{ZYX}^{(2)}\approx0
\end{gathered}
}
$$
